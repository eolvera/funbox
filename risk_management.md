## Visa and Mastercard Rules for Periodic Risk Reviews

Visa and Mastercard mandate periodic risk reviews to safeguard the payment ecosystem, as outlined in their operating regulations. Visa’s Merchant Data Standards and Mastercard’s Business Risk Assessment and Mitigation (BRAM) program require acquirers to monitor merchants for fraud, excessive chargebacks, and compliance violations. These reviews, typically conducted every 6-12 months for high-risk merchants, evaluate transaction patterns, business changes, and financial stability. The goal is to mitigate risks arising from shifts in merchant behavior, such as a retailer transitioning to high-ticket electronics, which may elevate chargeback risks. Visa’s Global Acquiring Risk Standards emphasize verifying merchant data, including ownership and MCC accuracy, to prevent misrepresentation. Similarly, Mastercard’s excessive chargeback program targets merchants exceeding 100 chargebacks and a 1.5% ratio monthly, triggering immediate reviews. A practical scenario involves a travel agency exhibiting a surge in card-not-present (CNP) transactions, potentially indicating bust-out fraud, where merchants process fictitious transactions before disappearing. Periodic reviews detect such anomalies, enabling acquirers to impose restrictions or terminate accounts. Compliance with PCI DSS and anti-fraud measures is also assessed, ensuring network security. Failure to conduct reviews can result in fines, reputational damage, or increased fraud losses. By enforcing periodic risk reviews, Visa and Mastercard maintain trust in the payment system, protect consumers, and ensure acquirers proactively manage merchant risks, aligning with industry standards for fraud prevention and compliance.

## AML Banking Rules and Best Practices for Ongoing Due Diligence

Anti-Money Laundering (AML) regulations, governed by the Bank Secrecy Act (BSA) and Financial Action Task Force (FATF) recommendations, require banks to implement ongoing due diligence to combat illicit finance. Beyond initial Know Your Customer (KYC) and Customer Identification Program (CIP) requirements under the USA PATRIOT Act, banks must continuously monitor customer accounts for suspicious activity. FATF’s risk-based approach mandates updating customer data, tracking transactions, and reassessing risk profiles, particularly for high-risk clients like politically exposed persons (PEPs). Ongoing due diligence involves automated transaction monitoring systems, periodic KYC reviews (every 12-24 months), and enhanced due diligence (EDD) for unusual patterns. For instance, a small business depositing cash just below $10,000 repeatedly may indicate structuring to evade Currency Transaction Reports (CTRs). Best practices include leveraging AI for anomaly detection, maintaining updated KYC records (e.g., business ownership or address), and documenting findings for regulatory audits, as emphasized by the OCC and FinCEN. A notable case is the 2018 Danske Bank scandal, where inadequate monitoring allowed $200 billion in laundered funds, underscoring the consequences of lax due diligence. Effective ongoing due diligence ensures compliance with AML laws, mitigates risks of money laundering, and protects the financial system’s integrity. By prioritizing thorough documentation and proactive monitoring, banks can file Suspicious Activity Reports (SARs) when necessary, fostering trust and regulatory compliance while aligning with your expertise in BSA/AML compliance and KYC/KYB due diligence.

## Known Fraud Schemes from CAMS Study Guide

The Certified Anti-Money Laundering Specialist (CAMS) study guide outlines several fraud schemes, including trade-based money laundering, bust-out fraud, and identity theft, which exploit financial systems and require robust AML controls. Trade-based money laundering manipulates trade documents to disguise illicit funds, such as over-invoicing exports to mask drug proceeds as legitimate trade profits. Red flags include mismatched invoice values or unusual shipping routes, detectable through transaction monitoring and trade finance audits. Bust-out fraud occurs when a merchant, often in retail, establishes credit with an acquirer, then processes fake transactions—e.g., $500,000 in CNP sales—before disappearing. Warning signs include sudden transaction spikes or high chargeback rates, which periodic reviews and velocity checks can identify. Identity theft involves stealing or fabricating personal data to open accounts or conduct transactions, such as using synthetic IDs to secure $10 million in fraudulent loans. The CAMS guide recommends stringent KYC and EDD to verify identities, particularly for online accounts, and behavioral analytics to detect anomalies. Financial institutions combat these schemes with layered defenses: comprehensive KYC, real-time transaction monitoring, and timely SAR filings. Staff training to recognize red flags, like inconsistent customer behavior, is critical, as is collaboration with FinCEN and law enforcement to trace schemes like smurfing or shell company abuse. These measures align with your expertise in fraud investigation and prevention, ensuring proactive risk management and compliance with AML standards to protect the financial system from sophisticated fraud.
